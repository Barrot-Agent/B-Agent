# Chapter 1: The Hiring Trap — Why More Work Used to Mean More People

Every service business converges on the same arithmetic. Demand arrives. You are
busy. Quality slips. You hire. For six weeks it feels like relief. Then the new
person needs work to do, so you sell more. Then they need managing, so you manage
less and sell less. Then the work outgrows them, so you hire again.

Nothing about that loop is stupid. For most of commercial history it was the only
loop available. Human hands were the only general-purpose executor, and human
hands cost a fixed amount per month whether or not the work arrived. The trap is
not that hiring is wrong. The trap is that hiring converts variable demand into
fixed cost, and fixed cost has to be fed whether or not the demand shows up.

## What a hire actually costs

The salary is the visible number, and it is the smallest part of the decision. The
fully loaded cost of a role is roughly:

```
fully loaded cost = salary
                  × (1 + payroll burden)      # taxes, benefits, insurance
                  + tooling and seats
                  + recruiting amortized over tenure
                  + management time
                  + ramp cost in the first 90 days
```

With `payroll burden = 0.20`, a $60,000 role costs $72,000 before you buy it a
laptop. Add 15% for recruiting amortized over two years, and you are at roughly
$81,000. Add the manager's time — if one manager of six spends a fifth of their
capacity per report, that is another 3–5% of the role — and the honest number is
close to $85,000 for a $60,000 hire. Every figure here is an assumption you should
replace with your own; the structure is what matters.

Now the part nobody puts in the spreadsheet: the hire is a **commitment with a
lag**. Recruiting takes 4–10 weeks. Ramp takes 8–16 weeks for knowledge work. So
the cost curve starts before the output curve, and the business carries both for a
quarter. If demand dips in month two, you cannot un-hire. You can only wait.

## The coordination tax

There is a second cost, and it scales faster than headcount. Two people doing
related work need a shared definition of done. Five need a meeting. Ten need a
process, a document, and someone whose job is the process.

This is the coordination tax: the fraction of each person's capacity that goes to
keeping other people aligned instead of doing the work. At three people it is
small enough to ignore. At eight it is a real line item. At fifteen it is the
reason the founder stops producing and starts attending.

The tax is not a management failure. It is what happens when the only shared
memory in the company is human. If two people must agree on how a task is done,
they must talk, and talking costs the most expensive hours in the building.

## Why the trap closes

The trap closes because of a specific asymmetry: **hiring is reversible only in
one direction.** You can add a person in a quarter. You can remove one in a
quarter, at a cost of severance, morale and knowledge. So every hire is a bet that
demand persists at or above the level that justified it.

In a market where demand is stable, that bet is fine. In a market where demand is
lumpy — project work, seasonal retail, fundraising cycles, anything sold to other
businesses — the bet is bad. You end up with capacity you cannot use and cost you
cannot cut, and the way you fix it is by selling harder, which adds coordination
tax, which lowers quality, which loses the customers you were selling to.

That is the loop. It is not a people problem. It is a **shape** problem: fixed
capacity against variable demand.

## The three ways out

There are only three ways out of the trap, and every service business picks one
whether it admits it or not.

**1. Raise price until demand matches capacity.** This works. It is also the
slowest, because it requires a brand, a niche and proof. Most operators try it
before they have any of the three.

**2. Constrain the offer until it is repeatable.** Narrow the service to a single
deliverable with a fixed definition of done, then productize it. This is what
agencies do when they stop taking "projects" and start selling "a landing page in
ten days." It converts variable work into a pipeline, and a pipeline can be staffed
against a forecast.

**3. Change the shape of capacity.** Instead of buying fixed human hours, buy
elastic execution: capacity that scales with demand, costs per unit, and does not
need a manager. For most of history this option did not exist for knowledge work.
It exists now, imperfectly, and it is the subject of this book.

Options 2 and 3 compound. A repeatable offer is exactly the thing that can be
delegated to software, because repeatability is what makes a job describable, and
a describable job is the only kind an agent can do.

## What the trap costs you personally

There is a version of this chapter that stays in the spreadsheet, and it misses the
point. The real cost of the hiring trap is not the payroll. It is what happens to
the founder's attention.

In the hiring model, your day is divided into three kinds of work: producing,
selling, and coordinating. Producing is what you are good at. Selling is what pays.
Coordinating is what grows. Every hire moves a slice of your day from the first
two into the third, permanently. The business gets bigger and your job gets worse.

The operators who escape are not the ones who work harder. They are the ones who
notice that most of the coordination exists to compensate for the fact that the
company has no shared, executable memory. Every process lives in someone's head.
Every standard is enforced by a person remembering it. Every handoff is a
conversation.

An agent with a written procedure is, among other things, a coordination
artificial: it does not need to be aligned in a meeting, because the procedure is
the alignment. That is a cold way to put it, and it is also the reason a solo
operator can now hold a workload that used to require a team.

## The honest counterargument

Agents are not people, and the differences matter. They do not build
relationships. They do not notice that a client is unhappy before the churn
email. They do not exercise judgment about which rule to break. They are
confident when wrong, and they fail in ways that look like success.

So the claim of this book is not "replace the team." The claim is narrower and
more useful: **a large class of expensive, repetitive, describable work can be
executed by software with a human on the approval line, at a marginal cost low
enough to change the shape of the business.** That class is bigger than most
operators think, and smaller than most vendors claim.

## Worksheet: the arithmetic of your own trap

Fill this in before you read another chapter. It takes twenty minutes and it
decides whether the rest of the book applies to you.

| Line | Your number | How to get it |
|---|---|---|
| Recurring hours per month | | Count the tasks that repeat, not the projects |
| Loaded hourly cost | | Salary × 1.25 ÷ 1,700 hours |
| Coordination hours per month | | Meetings + messages + context switching |
| Revenue per month | | Trailing three-month average |
| Revenue per producing hour | | Revenue ÷ (hours that produce) |
| Revenue per total hour | | Revenue ÷ all hours worked |
| Months of fixed cost in reserve | | Cash ÷ monthly fixed cost |

Two ratios do all the work. **Revenue per producing hour** tells you what your
time is worth when it is pointed at the customer. **Revenue per total hour** tells
you what the business actually pays you. The gap between them is the coordination
tax, expressed in money.

A worked example, with assumptions you should replace: 150 producing hours and 60
coordination hours a month, $30,000 revenue. Producing hour = $200. Total hour =
$143. The coordination tax is 29% of capacity, which is $8,500 a month of revenue
you did not produce because you were busy keeping the machine aligned.

Now the trigger question. If you added one person at a fully loaded $85,000 a year
— about $7,100 a month — how many hours of *producing* work would they have to
create before the hire pays for itself? At $200 per producing hour, 36 hours a
month. That sounds easy. It is not, because the new person also adds coordination
hours, and those come out of your producing capacity. The hire has to produce
their own 36 hours *and* replace the coordination hours they consume from you.

This is the number the hiring model never shows you, and it is why so many
businesses grow revenue and shrink margin at the same time.

Three questions to answer honestly:

1. What fraction of your month is coordination rather than production or sales?
2. Which of those coordination hours exist only because the process lives in
   someone's head?
3. If that process were written down and executable, how many hours would come
   back?

The answer to question three is your budget for the rest of this book.
