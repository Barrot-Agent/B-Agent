# Chapter 4: The $100K/Month Math — 50 Customers, One System, No Payroll

$100,000 a month is not a fantasy number and it is not a small number. It is
exactly the number where a service business stops being a freelancer's income and
starts being an asset, and it is low enough that one operator with a working agent
stack can reach it without a funding round.

There are only three shapes of it:

| Customers | Price / month | Revenue | What the buyer is buying |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 50 | $2,000 | $100,000 | one deliverable, one department's worth of relief |
| 20 | $5,000 | $100,000 | a function: pipeline, research desk, content engine |
| 5 | $20,000 | $100,000 | an outsourced department with a named owner |

All three are the same business with a different sales motion. The 50-row needs
volume marketing and self-serve onboarding. The 5-row needs two conversations and
a reference. Most operators should start at the 5-row, because five buyers can be
found by hand, and the margin at $20,000 a month per customer funds the machine
that makes the 50-row possible later.

## The only four numbers that matter

A service business with agents has a short income statement. Learn it and you can
price anything.

```
revenue            = customers × price
cost of delivery   = (deliverables × cost per deliverable) + infrastructure
gross margin       = (revenue - cost of delivery) / revenue
operating profit   = gross margin × revenue - your time - acquisition - tooling
```

Everything else is commentary. Now put real numbers in it.

## Worked example: the 50 × $2,000 case

Assumptions are labeled. Replace them with yours; the structure survives.

- 50 customers at $2,000/month → **$100,000 MRR**
- 300 deliverables per customer per month (about 10 per working day)
- 15,000 deliverables per month total
- Blended cost per deliverable, model plus tools: **$0.20** (Chapter 2 shows the
  arithmetic: a few cents of tokens, plus roughly ten cents of tool calls)
- Infrastructure: hosting, storage, observability, backups: **$300/month**
- Your time: 40 hours/week at a $150/hour opportunity cost: **$26,000/month**
- Tooling and subscriptions: **$500/month**
- Monthly churn 3% → 1.5 replacement customers → CAC $600 → **$900/month**

```
cost of delivery  = 15,000 × $0.20 + $300        = $3,300
gross margin      = (100,000 - 3,300) / 100,000  = 96.7%
operating profit  = 100,000 - 3,300 - 26,000 - 500 - 900 = $69,300
```

Read that last line carefully. The business grosses 96.7% and nets 69% **while
paying the operator a full market salary for their own time**. That is the whole
argument. The constraint on this business is not compute. It is the operator's
attention and the customer's willingness to pay $2,000.

## The binding constraint is exceptions, not tokens

This is the part that separates a real model from a pitch deck. Your capacity is
not set by how many deliverables the system can produce. It is set by how many of
them need a human.

Take the same 15,000 monthly deliverables and vary the exception rate, assuming
6 minutes of operator time per exception:

| Exception rate | Exceptions / month | Operator hours | Verdict |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1% | 150 | 15 h | comfortable |
| 2% | 300 | 30 h | comfortable |
| 5% | 750 | 75 h | half your month |
| 8% | 1,200 | 120 h | at the ceiling of one person |
| 12% | 1,800 | 180 h | the business now owns you |

At 8% you are spending 120 of your ~173 working hours a month on exceptions. There
is no room left for sales, and the business stops growing while appearing to work.
At 12% you are worse off than doing the job yourself, because you also have to
maintain the system.

So the highest-leverage engineering work in this business is not making the agent
smarter. It is **moving the exception rate from 8% to 3%**, and then automating the
review of the remaining 3%. Every chapter in Part Five is about that.

## Sensitivity: where the model breaks

A single-point forecast is a lie with a decimal. Here is the same business at
different prices and volumes, holding cost per deliverable constant.

| Price | Customers for $100K | Deliverables/month | Delivery cost | Gross margin |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $500 | 200 | 60,000 | $12,300 | 87.7% |
| $1,000 | 100 | 30,000 | $6,300 | 93.7% |
| $2,000 | 50 | 15,000 | $3,300 | 96.7% |
| $5,000 | 20 | 6,000 | $1,500 | 98.5% |
| $20,000 | 5 | 1,500 | $600 | 99.4% |

The margin column flatters the high-price rows. The column that decides your life
is the second one: **how many customers you must find and keep.** Two hundred
customers at $500 is a marketing company. Five customers at $20,000 is a
consultancy with a software back end. Same revenue, completely different business,
completely different skills required from you.

Choose the row you can sell, then engineer the cost.

## Acquisition, honestly

The number that kills agent businesses is not delivery cost. It is customer
acquisition, because the offer is unfamiliar and the buyer is skeptical.

A cold outbound model with assumptions stated: 1,000 researched contacts, 4%
reply rate, 20% of replies become calls, 25% of calls close. That is 40 replies,
8 calls, 2 customers. To add 2 customers a month you need 1,000 quality contacts
a month, which is itself a job an agent can do — and that is exactly why the sales
agent in Chapter 14 comes before the pricing chapter.

With 3% monthly churn you lose 1.5 customers a month at 50 customers. So 2 closes
a month is net growth of half a customer. Thin. This is why retention beats
acquisition in this model: a 1% churn improvement at 50 customers is worth more
than a 20% improvement in close rate, and it is cheaper to produce.

## The three rules that keep the math honest

**Rule one: price at 3–5× fully loaded cost, not at cost plus a feeling.** If a
deliverable costs you $0.20 in compute and 20 minutes of review, your cost is not
$0.20. It is 20 minutes times your rate plus $0.20. Price against that.

**Rule two: never let one customer exceed 30% of revenue.** At 5 customers, one
churn is a 20% revenue event. At 50, it is a rounding error. Concentration is the
hidden risk in the high-price rows.

**Rule three: reinvest one agent's margin into the next agent.** The first
employee funds the second. The second funds the third. This is the only growth
loop in the model that does not require you to hire.

## What $100K is not

It is not $100K in your pocket. In the worked example, $69,300 is what remains
after paying yourself a market rate, and that is before tax. It is also not
recurring by default: service revenue churns unless the deliverable becomes
infrastructure the customer depends on. The operators who compound are the ones who
convert a monthly deliverable into a monthly dependency — the report the board
reads, the pipeline the sales team works, the ledger the accountant closes against.

## Three scenarios, not one forecast

The worked example above is the base case. Any number in it can move, so here are
three versions with the same cost structure. The point is not to predict; it is to
see which input your survival depends on.

| Input | Conservative | Base | Aggressive |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Customers | 30 | 50 | 80 |
| Price | $1,500 | $2,000 | $2,500 |
| Deliverables / customer / month | 200 | 300 | 300 |
| Cost per deliverable | $0.20 | $0.20 | $0.30 |
| Exception rate | 6% | 3% | 2% |
| Operator hours / month | 173 | 173 | 220 |
| Operator hours on exceptions | 108 | 54 | 30 |
| **Revenue** | **$45,000** | **$100,000** | **$200,000** |
| Delivery cost | $1,500 | $3,300 | $7,500 |
| Gross margin | 96.7% | 96.7% | 96.3% |
| Profit after your time | $19,900 | $69,300 | $150,500 |

Look at the conservative column. Revenue is 45% lower but profit is 71% lower,
because the operator's time is fixed while revenue is not. That is the leverage
curve in one line: **below a certain volume, the operator's own hours are the
largest cost in the business, and no amount of automation changes that until
volume arrives.**

Now look at the exception rows. In the conservative case the operator spends 108
hours a month cleaning up 6% of 6,000 deliverables. That is the entire working
month, which means sales stops, which means the conservative case stays
conservative forever. The base case spends 54 hours — one third of capacity — and
keeps the other two thirds for selling and improving. The difference between the
two is not price or volume. It is an exception rate that moved from 6% to 3%.

## The diagnostic when margin is fine and life is not

Gross margin in this model is almost always above 90%, which makes it a useless
management metric. When an operator says the business is not working, they mean one
of four things, and each has a different fix.

**"I have no time to sell."** Count exception hours. If they exceed 40% of
capacity, the fix is quality engineering (Chapters 28 and 29), not another hire.

**"Customers keep leaving."** Compute churn by cohort. If month-three retention is
below 85%, the deliverable is not becoming a dependency. The fix is scope, not
price — make the output something the customer's own process runs on.

**"I'm busy but revenue is flat."** Price is too low or customer count is too high
for your capacity. Move up one row of the price table in Chapter 4 and drop the
bottom 20% of customers by margin.

**"It works but I hate it."** The offer has drifted into work you do not want to
supervise. This is legitimate. Pick a different job, even at lower revenue; a
business you resent will be run badly.

## The twelve-week cash plan

One operator, no funding, first employee. The sequence that works:

- **Weeks 1–2:** build and pilot the first employee on your own work. Cost: your
  time, plus under $100 in API spend.
- **Weeks 3–4:** deliver for one design-partner customer at 50% of the target
  price, in exchange for a reference and weekly feedback.
- **Weeks 5–8:** fix the exception rate until it is under 5%. Do not add customers.
  Every new customer before this point multiplies the error you have not yet fixed.
- **Weeks 9–12:** raise to full price for new customers, keep the design partner at
  the discounted rate for two more months, and start the second employee with the
  margin from the first.

Twelve weeks to one employee at full price. That is slower than the pitch and
faster than hiring.
