# Chapter 6: What Every Founder Needs to Know Before Replacing Headcount

The phrase "replacing headcount" is the wrong frame, and it causes most of the
mistakes. Nobody employs a person to do one task. You employ a person to hold a
bundle: produce the work, notice when it is wrong, talk to the customer, remember
the exception, and show up tomorrow. An agent can take a large share of the
producing and a small share of the rest.

So the decision is not "person or bot." It is: **which tasks inside this bundle can
leave the human, and what has to stay.** Get that granularity right and the rest is
engineering. Get it wrong and you will either move too little (and see no return)
or too much (and lose a customer).

## The readiness score

Score each criterion 0, 1 or 2. A job is ready for supervised autonomy at 9 or
above, ready for assisted execution at 6, and not ready below 6.

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Definable** | nobody can say what "done" means | a person could explain it | there is a written spec and two sample outputs |
| **Repetitive** | one-off | monthly | weekly or more, same shape |
| **Measurable** | quality is a vibe | a human spot-checks | a machine can check output against rules |
| **Reversible** | money or legal consequence | customer-visible | internal and undoable |
| **Non-relational** | the work is the relationship | some customer contact | no customer contact |
| **Data-clean** | inputs vary wildly per case | a few formats | one structured input |

Two things about this table are counterintuitive.

First, **"definable" is worth more than "easy."** A hard job with a precise spec
automates better than an easy job with a vague one. The classic mistake is starting
with something simple that nobody has ever documented, then discovering that the
reason it was easy for a human is that the human was silently making fifty
judgment calls.

Second, **reversibility dominates everything else.** A 95%-accurate agent on a
reversible task is a productivity gain. A 99%-accurate agent on an irreversible
task is a liability, because the one percent arrives as a refund, a compliance
letter or a public post.

## The five questions that must be answered before the first run

**1. Who owns the output?** Name a human. Not a team, not "the operator" in the
abstract — a name. If the answer is "the agent," you have built a system with no
accountability, and the first serious error will be handled by whoever is closest
to the customer.

**2. What is the error budget?** In numbers. "Two percent of items may require
manual correction; zero may contain a wrong number in a customer-facing total."
Write both halves: the tolerated rate and the never-event. The never-event is what
the approval gate protects.

**3. What does the agent cost per unit, fully loaded?** Model, tools, storage,
review time, and the maintenance hours you will spend fixing prompts. If the
number is not lower than the human cost by at least 3×, the automation is a hobby.

**4. What happens on day one of a provider outage?** Not "it won't happen." What
does the business do for four hours when the API returns errors? If the answer is
"nothing works," you have built a single point of failure with a monthly fee.

**5. Who is allowed to change the rules?** The fence (Chapter 12) is only a fence
if changing it requires a deliberate act. If any prompt edit can widen permissions,
you do not have governance, you have a suggestion.

## The legal and ethical floor

This is not legal advice. It is a list of the things you must take to a lawyer
before you scale, because they are jurisdiction-specific and expensive to get
wrong.

- **Employment law.** Automating tasks is generally a business decision; changing
  a person's role, pay or status is an employment-law event. The two are not the
  same conversation, and treating them as one is how companies end up in a dispute
  they would have avoided with a month of planning.
- **Contractor classification.** If you replace an employee with a contractor who
  is directed like an employee, many jurisdictions will reclassify. The agent in
  the middle does not change that analysis.
- **Data protection.** Sending customer data to a model provider is a data transfer.
  You need a lawful basis, a retention rule, and a way to delete. "It's in the
  prompt" is not a retention policy.
- **Regulated advice.** Legal, medical, financial and tax outputs carry licensing
  and liability rules that no amount of accuracy solves. Sell the preparation, not
  the advice.
- **Disclosure.** Where a customer reasonably expects a human, say what is
  automated. The businesses that hide it spend the savings on reputation repair.

## The three failure modes to design against

**The silent error.** The agent produces a plausible wrong answer, the check passes
because the check was written by the same model, and the customer finds it. Design
against it with an independent verifier — code that validates structure and
arithmetic, not another model opinion — and with sampled human review that is
logged.

**Permission creep.** The agent starts read-only. A busy week adds write access "just
for this task." Six months later it can move money. Design against it with scoped
credentials, a written allowlist, and a review cadence that forces the list to be
re-justified.

**The accountability vacuum.** Everyone assumes someone is watching the dashboard.
Nobody is. Design against it with a named owner per agent, a weekly exception
review, and an alert that reaches a phone rather than a channel.

## What to do with the person

Three options, in order of preference.

**Redeploy.** The tasks that remain are usually the higher-value ones: the
relationship, the exception, the design of the next procedure. A capable person
moved from production to specification is worth more than they were before,
because specification is the scarce skill in an agent-run business.

**Upskill into the fence.** Somebody has to own quality: sample audits, error
taxonomy, regression tests when a skill changes. That is a real job and it is
usually filled by the person who used to do the work, because they are the only one
who knows what "wrong" looks like.

**Reduce.** If neither applies, reduce deliberately: one conversation, a fair
package, a transition plan, and no surprises. The cost of doing this badly is not
legal, it is cultural — the people who stay are watching how you treat the people
who go.

## The 30-day pilot that decides everything

Do not roll out. Pilot.

1. Pick one job that scores 9 or above.
2. Collect 50 real items, including the ugly ones.
3. Write the definition of done and the never-events.
4. Run the agent on all 50 in a sandbox. No customer sees anything.
5. Score every output yourself. Count: correct, correct-with-edit, wrong-caught,
   wrong-shipped.
6. Compute the exception rate and the operator minutes per item.
7. Compare against the human baseline on the same 50 items.
8. Decide: scale, iterate, or stop.

The gate is simple. Ship if the exception rate is under your budget, the operator
minutes are under a third of the human baseline, and zero never-events occurred. If
any of the three fails, iterate once. If it fails twice, the job is not ready and
the honest move is to pick a different one.

## The pilot scorecard

The pilot is only a decision if it produces a scorecard. Fill this in for the 50
items, then apply the rule. Do not average the outcomes; count them.

| Field | Count | Definition |
|---|---:|---|
| Items run | 50 | all real, including the ugly ones |
| Correct, no edit | | shipped as produced |
| Correct after a small edit | | under 2 minutes of operator time |
| Wrong, caught before shipping | | the verifier or the operator stopped it |
| Wrong, shipped | | reached a customer or a downstream system |
| Never-events | | the things your spec says may never happen |
| Operator minutes total | | stopwatch, not estimate |
| Cost, model + tools | | from the provider dashboard, not a guess |

Two derived numbers decide it:

```
exception rate   = (edits + wrong-caught + wrong-shipped) / items run
minutes per item = operator minutes / items run
```

And two absolute gates sit outside the arithmetic:

- **Never-events must be zero.** One wrong number in a customer-facing total is not
  a rate problem; it is a stop signal. Fix the verifier and re-run the pilot.
- **Minutes per item must be under one third of the human baseline.** If a person
  takes 12 minutes and the agent plus review takes 5, you have a business. If it
  takes 9, you have a slightly cheaper person who cannot be trained.

A realistic first pilot fails. Suppose 50 items produce 38 correct, 6 small edits,
3 wrong-caught, 2 wrong-shipped, and one of the wrong-shipped was a duplicated
invoice. Exception rate is 22%, and the never-event gate failed. That is not a
reason to abandon the job; it is the reason the pilot exists. The fix is almost
never "a better model." It is a mechanical check — a duplicate key, a required
field, a cross-file total — and a rule that the agent may not write a final value
without the check passing.

Re-run after the fix. Second pilot: 44 correct, 4 edits, 2 wrong-caught, 0 shipped,
0 never-events. Exception rate 12%, minutes per item 2.4 against a human baseline
of 12. Scale.

## The one-page decision memo

Before you scale, write a page that answers five questions in this order:

1. What job, in one sentence, with the artifact named?
2. What is the human baseline, in minutes and dollars, measured on the same items?
3. What is the agent cost per item, including review?
4. What is the exception rate, and what is the budget?
5. Who is accountable when it is wrong, by name?

If any answer is missing, the pilot is not finished. If all five are present, you
have something rarer than a working agent: a defensible decision.
