# Chapter 2: The Grok Bot Era — The Moment Software Started Doing the Job

The change is easiest to see as a list of what an agent now touches. According to
the Grok Bot documentation as of November 2026, a Bot is an agent with access to a
real computer in the cloud — a browser, a filesystem and a terminal — that works
across apps and websites, can run on a schedule, remembers your preferences and
logins, can be reached by chat or voice, and can hand work to another Bot. You
connect it to the tools you already use, and it supports custom tooling through
MCP or a plain API.

Read that list again and notice what is not on it: "answers questions." That is
the smallest thing on the list, and it is the only thing the previous generation
could do.

## Three shifts, in order of importance

**Shift one: the unit of work became an artifact.** A chatbot produces text in a
window. An agent produces a file, a draft in your Drive, a message in a thread, a
calendar event, a deck in your template, a spreadsheet with the numbers already
reconciled. The difference sounds cosmetic until you count what happens after the
answer. In the chat model, you are the integration layer: you read, you copy, you
paste, you format, you send. That work is invisible in every demo and it is most
of the time.

**Shift two: the agent has a computer that persists.** A browser with your
sessions, a filesystem that keeps state between runs, a terminal that can run the
script it just wrote. Persistence is what turns a task into a job. Without it,
every run starts from zero and the agent cannot recover its own work. With it, an
agent can start something on Monday and finish it on Wednesday.

**Shift three: it runs when you are not there.** Routines and schedules turn an
agent from a tool you operate into an employee you supervise. "Every Sunday
evening, read the week ahead, flag clashes, send a plan for Monday" is a different
category of object than "summarize my calendar." One is a command. The other is a
job with a cadence, and cadence is what produces compounding output.

## Why this is not RPA with better marketing

Robotic process automation was the previous attempt at the same promise, and it
failed in a specific, instructive way. RPA drove the interface: it clicked the
button at coordinates, it read the field by its position, it depended on the screen
looking the same tomorrow. When the vendor shipped a redesign, the bot broke. The
maintenance cost ate the savings, and the industry learned to call this "brittleness."

Agentic execution is brittle in a different place. It reads the page the way a
person does, which survives a redesign but can be fooled by a confusing layout. It
writes the file using the file's own semantics, which survives a rename but can
produce a plausible wrong value. The failure moved from **mechanical** to
**semantic**. That is progress, and it changes what you have to monitor: you no
longer watch for a crashed selector, you watch for a confident wrong answer.

This is the single most important operational consequence in the book. A system
that fails loudly is cheap to run. A system that fails quietly needs a test suite,
a sample audit, and a human on the approval line for anything irreversible.

## The cost structure changed shape

The old model priced a seat. You paid per person per month, and marginal work was
free until it wasn't — until the person was saturated and you needed another seat.

The agent model prices the work. Current documented rates for the flagship text
model are $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens below a
200K-token prompt, with the coding model at $1 per million in and $2 per million
out. Server-side tools are priced separately — web search at $5 per thousand
calls, code execution at $5 per thousand calls, document collections at $2.50 per
thousand.

Put those numbers into a real task. A research memo that reads 10,000 tokens of
source material and writes 2,000 tokens of output costs:

```
input  10,000 × $2.00 / 1,000,000 = $0.020
output  2,000 × $6.00 / 1,000,000 = $0.012
                              sum  = $0.032
```

Three cents. Add twenty web searches:

```
20 calls × $5.00 / 1,000 = $0.10
```

Now the task costs thirteen cents, and the searches dominate. This ratio — cheap
tokens, expensive tools — is the thing to internalize, because it inverts the
usual optimization instinct. The expensive part of an agent run is rarely the
thinking. It is the number of times it goes out to the world.

Two implications follow, and both become chapters later. First, cache and batch:
prompt caching cuts repeated context cost, and the batch API discounts most text
models by 20% for work that can wait. Second, retrieve less: a connector that
returns eight relevant records beats a search that returns eighty, and the
difference is measured in cents per call, which multiplies into dollars per
customer per month.

## The demo trap

Every capability in this chapter can be demonstrated in ten minutes and will
impress a room. That is the danger. A demo shows the happy path on data chosen by
the demonstrator. A business runs on the unhappy path, on data chosen by the
customer, at 3 a.m., when the API returns a 429 and the agent has to decide whether
to retry, wait, or escalate.

The operators who win with this stack are not the ones with the best demo. They
are the ones who wrote down, before the first run, what a correct output looks
like and what an incorrect one looks like, then measured the gap on fifty real
items. That measurement is unglamorous and it is the entire moat, because your
competitor can copy the prompt in an afternoon but cannot copy your sample of
what "good" means in your customer's business.

## The three tests for "is this job in the era"

Not every job belongs to this moment. Before you build anything, apply three
tests:

1. **Artifact test.** Does the job end in a thing — a file, a record, a message,
   a booking — that a machine can check? If the output is "better judgment," stop.
2. **Repetition test.** Does it happen at least weekly, with substantially the
   same shape each time? A one-off is a project, not a job.
3. **Reversibility test.** If the agent gets it wrong, can you undo it before a
   customer notices? If not, the work can still be automated, but it must sit
   behind an approval, and the approval must be cheap enough that you will
   actually do it.

Jobs that pass all three are the class this book is about. Jobs that pass two are
candidates for partial automation. Jobs that pass one are a distraction wearing a
prompt.

## What to price when tools dominate

If tokens are cheap and tool calls are not, then the thing you must design around
is **how often the agent goes out to the world**. Three archetypes cover most
service work, and they have very different cost shapes.

| Archetype | Typical run | Token cost | Tool cost | Total |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Transform (draft, summarize, format) | 8K in / 2K out, no tools | $0.028 | $0.00 | **$0.03** |
| Retrieve-and-synthesize (research memo) | 12K in / 2K out, 20 searches | $0.036 | $0.10 | **$0.14** |
| Act-and-verify (ops task, 4 writes + 4 reads) | 6K in / 1K out, 8 calls | $0.018 | $0.04 + tool pricing | **$0.06+** |

The middle row is the one that surprises people. A research memo costs four times
as much as a drafting task, and three quarters of that cost is search. If you sell
research by the memo, your margin is decided by whether the agent reads eight
sources or eighty.

Three habits follow, and they are engineering habits, not prompt habits:

**Cap the calls, not the words.** A per-run ceiling on web searches does more for
your margin than any instruction about being concise. When the ceiling is hit, the
agent must either answer with what it has or escalate — both are better than
silently spending.

**Cache the stable part.** Pricing documents, brand guides and customer context
should be identical across runs so the provider can serve them from cache. Cached
input is billed at a fraction of fresh input on the documented rate card. The
practical rule: put the variable data at the end of the prompt, the stable
instructions at the beginning, and never reorder them.

**Batch what can wait.** Anything that is not customer-facing in real time — nightly
digests, weekly reports, bulk classification — can go through the batch API, which
is documented at a 20% discount on the text models that support it. A 20% cut on a
$3,000 monthly inference bill is $600, which is a month of infrastructure.

One more number worth internalizing: **retries**. A failed run that is retried
three times costs four times as much and produces one output. If your retry policy
is "try again until it works," your unit economics are set by your worst-behaved
input, not your average one. Cap retries at two, log the failure, escalate.
