# Chapter 11: Grok Swarm — Scale With Multiple Agents

One agent is a worker. Several agents with handoffs are a team, and a team is how
you get through a week of work that no single context window can hold.

A word of precision first, because this book has a rule about not inventing
products. "Grok Swarm" is not a documented product name. What *is* documented is
the capability: multiple Bots can work together, like a real team, on tasks that
would otherwise take hours or days; one Bot's routine can pull the week's numbers,
another can draft a campaign from them, a third can review it, and the bundle
arrives for your approval. On the API side there is a documented multi-agent model,
`grok-4.20-multi-agent-0309`, priced like the other 4.20 text models. So this chapter
treats "swarm" as an architecture you build from those primitives, not as a SKU you
buy.

That distinction matters commercially: the thing you sell is the output of the
team, and the thing you own is the wiring between them.

## Why more than one agent

There are four legitimate reasons to split a job across agents, and only four.

**Context isolation.** A research job that reads 300 pages cannot keep them all in
one context without paying the long-context cliff and degrading attention. Split by
source, summarize per source, synthesize at the end.

**Specialization.** A drafting agent with a voice guide and a review agent with a
rubric produce better work than one agent asked to do both, because the reviewer
cannot see the reasoning that produced the draft and therefore is not anchored by
it.

**Parallelism.** Ten independent items take one-tenth the wall-clock time if ten
agents run at once. This matters for delivery deadlines, not for cost.

**Separation of authority.** The agent that proposes a payment and the agent that
approves it should not be the same agent, for the same reason a person does not
approve their own expenses.

Note what is not on the list: "it sounds more advanced." Every additional agent adds
a handoff, and every handoff is a place where information is lost and cost is
multiplied.

## Four topologies that work

| Topology | Shape | Use it when | Cost multiplier |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Pipeline | produce → review → approve | quality matters, one artifact | 2–3× |
| Fan-out | N workers, one synthesizer | independent sources or items | N + 1× |
| Specialist pool | router sends task by type | mixed queue of job types | 1.2–2× |
| Reviewer pair | producer + adversarial checker | factual claims, high stakes | 2× |

**Pipeline** is the workhorse. The producer drafts, the reviewer checks against a
rubric, the human approves the bundle. It is the documented pattern in Grok Bot and
the one that most resembles how a good agency actually works.

**Fan-out** is for volume. Ten agents each research one competitor, one synthesizer
writes the comparison. The synthesizer is the bottleneck and the quality ceiling;
give it the strongest model in the stack.

**Specialist pool** is a routing problem, not an agent problem. A triage agent
classifies incoming work and hands it to the right specialist. Keep the router dumb
and deterministic where you can; a model-based router that misclassifies sends work
to the wrong employee and nobody notices until the output is wrong.

**Reviewer pair** is the cheapest quality upgrade in the book. One agent produces,
another tries to falsify the top three claims. It roughly doubles cost and catches
the errors that matter. Do not let the reviewer see the producer's reasoning — only
the artifact and the rubric — or it will rationalize instead of check.

## Three topologies that don't

**Unbounded debate.** Agents argue until they agree. This burns cost, converges on
the most confident agent rather than the most correct one, and produces no
audit trail.

**Voting on facts.** Three agents vote on what the revenue was. Facts are not
opinions; they come from a source of truth. If the agents disagree, the answer is
"query the ledger," not "majority wins."

**Recursive delegation with no budget.** Agent A asks Agent B, which asks Agent C,
which asks A. Each hop looks reasonable. The bill does not. Every delegation needs a
depth limit and a cost ceiling.

## The handoff contract

The single biggest determinant of swarm quality is what gets passed between agents.
Prose handoffs lose information; structured handoffs survive.

```json
{
  "task_id": "cmp-2026-11-brief",
  "from": "research",
  "to": "draft",
  "artifact": {
    "path": "work/brief-2026-11.md",
    "sha256": "c41f...",
    "schema": "brief/v2"
  },
  "confidence": 0.72,
  "claims": [
    {"claim": "Competitor A raised prices in October", "source": "https://...", "date": "2026-10-04"},
    {"claim": "Category search volume fell 12% QoQ", "source": "internal/warehouse", "date": "2026-11-01"}
  ],
  "open_questions": ["No data on competitor B's enterprise tier"],
  "escalate_if": "any claim lacks a source or is older than 90 days"
}
```

Four fields do the work. **`artifact.sha256`** proves the receiver is working on the
intended version. **`confidence`** lets the next agent decide how hard to verify.
**`claims` with sources** makes the reviewer's job mechanical. **`open_questions`**
prevents the next agent from silently filling gaps with plausible invention.

A handoff without sources is an opinion transfer, and opinions compound into
confident errors.

## The math of a hop

Every additional agent multiplies both cost and latency. A concrete comparison for
a 2,000-word brief:

| Configuration | Calls | Tokens (approx) | Cost (approx) | Wall clock |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Single agent | 1 | 14K in / 3K out | $0.046 | 1× |
| Producer + reviewer | 2 | 30K in / 6K out | $0.096 | 2× |
| Fan-out 5 + synthesizer | 6 | 70K in / 10K out | $0.200 | 2.5× |
| Fan-out 5 + reviewer + approver | 8 | 90K in / 12K out | $0.252 | 3× |

The numbers are small in absolute terms, which is exactly why operators over-swarm:
at these prices, tripling the agent count feels free. But the *failure surface*
triples too, and the operator's review time does not shrink — it moves from checking
one artifact to reconciling three.

The rule: **add an agent only when you can name the failure it prevents.** "Better
quality" is not a failure. "Catches an unsourced claim before the customer sees it"
is.

## The primary-Bot pattern

When several agents are working, the operator's attention becomes the bottleneck.
The documented answer is a primary Bot that can answer "what is everyone doing" on
behalf of the roster.

Use it as a status aggregator, not as a supervisor. Ask it: what ran, what is
blocked, what needs approval, what failed. Then go to the specific agent for detail.
This is the difference between managing a team and polling five chat windows.

Keep the aggregator read-only with respect to the other agents' work. A supervisor
that can cancel or reassign introduces a new class of failure — an agent deciding
another agent's job is unimportant.

## Failure modes specific to teams

**Lost state.** Agent B does not see Agent A's intermediate file because the working
directory differs. The handoff contract's path and hash solve this; verify the hash
on receipt.

**Approval ambiguity.** Two agents both think the other will ask for approval. The
documented behaviour is that an approval controls the *proposed action* of a specific
agent, so each consequential action needs its own gate. Never rely on "the pipeline
asks somewhere."

**Groupthink.** Three agents from the same model family reviewing each other's work
share the same blind spots. Use a different model, a different prompt, or — best —
a mechanical checker for the claims that can be checked mechanically.

**Silent divergence.** Two agents produce two versions of the same artifact and a
third picks one. Version every artifact, name the winner explicitly in the handoff,
and archive the loser.

## A worked pipeline, end to end

The job: a weekly competitor brief, eight sources, 2,000 words, delivered Monday
morning. Four workers, one artifact, one approval. The whole run fits on a page:

```
routine   Mon 06:00   research-bot  read sources, write work/brief-2026-11.md
handoff   structured  research -> draft
bot       draft-bot   write out/brief.md from the artifact, never from chat history
handoff   structured  draft -> review
bot       review-bot  falsify the top three claims, write out/review.json
gate      approval    operator sees one card: publish, revise, or discard
promote   code        copy out/brief.md to the client folder, append the receipt
```

Five details make that diagram work, and each one is a failure that was moved out of
the run and into the wiring.

**The research Bot never writes the brief.** It writes a source pack: one section per
source, each with a URL, a date, and a two-sentence summary. Separating collection
from composition means a bad source is visible as a bad source instead of as an
awkward paragraph nobody can trace.

**The draft Bot reads the artifact, not the conversation.** Chat history is a
convenient way to lose the version you meant. The handoff names a path and a hash, so
the drafting step is reproducible from disk alone — which is also what makes it
debuggable three weeks later.

**The reviewer writes JSON, not prose.** A review that says "looks solid, maybe check
the pricing claim" is a message. A review that says
`{"claim": "...", "verdict": "unsupported", "reason": "source is 14 months old"}`
is a gate. The first one gets skimmed; the second one can be enforced.

**The approval card is raised by the pipeline, not by an agent.** The card shows the
artifact path, the diff since last week, and the reviewer's objections. Automation
that asks for approval through an agent's chat message will eventually ask in the
wrong place.

**Promotion is a code path.** The last step is a short deterministic script that
copies `out/brief.md` after the review verdict is clean. No model is involved in the
decision to publish.

Cost for the run, at documented rates, with the strong model on planning, drafting and
review, and the cheap model on per-source extraction:

| Stage | Model | Tokens in / out | Cost | Wall clock |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Research (8 sources) | `grok-build-0.1` | 60K / 8K | $0.076 | 4 min |
| Draft | `grok-4.7` | 18K / 4K | $0.060 | 2 min |
| Review (3 claims) | `grok-4.7` | 12K / 2K | $0.036 | 1 min |
| Web searches (24) | tool | — | $0.120 | — |
| **Total** | | | **$0.29** | **~7 min** |

Twenty-nine cents and seven minutes of wall clock, with a human gate at the end. The
same brief from a freelancer is two hundred dollars and three days; the same brief
from one heroic agent call is cheaper still, and wrong in a way nobody catches because
there is only one voice in the room.

## Debugging a swarm that produced a bad artifact

A pipeline fails differently from a single agent, and the difference is good news:
you can bisect it.

The bad output is a number that is off by a factor of ten in paragraph four. Do not
start by reading prompts. Start at the artifact and walk backwards.

**Step 1 — locate the claim in the handoff chain.** Find which stage first asserted
the number. In a structured handoff this is a grep across `claims`; in a prose
pipeline it is an afternoon. This is the argument for the contract in one sentence.

**Step 2 — replay the hop, not the pipeline.** Take the recorded input artifact, its
hash, and the prompt version, and re-run that single stage. If it reproduces the
error, the fault is local: the prompt, the tool result, or the model. If it does not
reproduce, the fault is upstream — the input was already contaminated, or the run saw
a different version than the one on disk.

**Step 3 — check the tool result before the reasoning.** The most common cause of a
confidently wrong number in a research pipeline is a search that returned a page
about a different company, a different year, or a different currency. The model then
reasons correctly from a wrong premise. Model quality is not the variable; retrieval
is.

**Step 4 — check the units and the period.** A revenue figure in thousands read as
millions is a factor-of-1000 error that survives every fluent sentence around it. A
schema that names units and periods at the field level prevents the entire class.

**Step 5 — only then question the model.** If the input was right, the tool result was
right, and the arithmetic was done in code, the remaining cause is judgment: the agent
chose a defensible-sounding interpretation that is not yours. That is a skill bug, not
a model bug — the rule you would have applied was never written down. Fix the skill,
add the case to the golden set, and the error cannot come back silently.

Three practices make this fast enough to be worth doing:

- **Every stage writes its input hash into its receipt.** Then "which version did this
  run see" is a lookup rather than an investigation.
- **Keep the raw tool results for the retention window.** Reconstructing a search from
  a summary is guesswork; the summary is exactly where the error was introduced.
- **Log the model and prompt version per call.** A pipeline that improved after an
  upgrade and a pipeline that broke after one look identical without this field.

The debugging rule that generalizes: **in a pipeline, the artifact is the evidence and
the transcript is hearsay.** Read the files.

## The operating cadence

A swarm is not a build, it is an operation, and operations need a rhythm. Four
cadences cover a one-person company without turning management into a second job.

**Daily, five minutes: the approval queue.** Every card that expired, every run that
escalated, every artifact that failed validation. You are not reviewing the work; you
are looking for patterns in the exceptions.

**Weekly, thirty minutes: the golden set.** Run each skill's ten cases and diff. A
changed output after a model upgrade is expected; an unexplained changed output is a
finding.

**Monthly, one hour: cost per successful artifact.** Not total spend — cost divided by
artifacts that passed validation. Total spend rewards doing less work; this number
rewards doing work that lands.

**Quarterly, two hours: remove an agent.** Name the topology and ask which stage
earned its cost. The reviewer usually did. The second synthesizer usually did not.
Deleting a stage is the only reliable way to find out whether it was load-bearing, and
a team that only ever grows is a team with a cost problem and no way to see it.

The failure this cadence prevents is specific. A pipeline where every stage is
individually reasonable drifts into a pipeline where three stages exist because of an
incident nobody remembers, each adding latency and a handoff. Nobody decides to build
that; it accumulates. The quarterly deletion is the decision that reverses it.

## When not to swarm

Do not split a job when:

- one agent can hold the whole thing in context comfortably;
- the task is strictly sequential and fast (handoff overhead exceeds the work);
- error attribution matters more than speed (a single agent's failure is easier to
  trace than a pipeline's);
- you cannot yet describe the job well enough for one agent (Chapter 9 first).

The last one is the common case. A team of agents executing a vague procedure
produces a vague result at three times the cost. Fix the skill, then scale it.

governance, and the approval boundary that turns autonomy into something you can
